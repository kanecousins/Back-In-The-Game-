Step 1

Recognise

Is this a pars stress injury? Repeated extension and extension with rotation stress the pars, most often at L5 then L4.

Three things that raise suspicion

1Adolescent athlete with activity-related low back pain
2Repeated extension, rotation or impact
3Recent load spike or growth spurt

Insidious, eased by rest, may refer to the gluteal region, and often treated as a strain first. None of this is diagnostic.

Loading patterns

Pattern Examples
Repeated arching Gymnastics, dance, diving, butterfly
Extension with rotation Fast bowling, pitching, tennis serve, golf
Impact into extension Gymnastics landings, jumping sports
Heavy axial load in extension Weightlifting, rowing, football linemen

The spectrum

Stage Imaging What it means
Stress reaction MRI oedema, cortex intact Highest healing potential
Incomplete fracture Partial cortical break (CT) Treat as active injury
Complete acute fracture Cortical breach with oedema Active; healing possible, not required for recovery
Chronic non-union Sclerotic margins, no oedema Union unlikely; may not be the pain source
With slip Forward vertebral slip Different entity: specialist grading first

Defects are common

About 6% of adults have a pars defect, most without symptoms (Fredrickson et al., 1984). With conservative care, 92% of athletes return to sport (89% to pre-injury level). That figure is Level IV and cannot compare pathways (Grazina et al., 2019).
